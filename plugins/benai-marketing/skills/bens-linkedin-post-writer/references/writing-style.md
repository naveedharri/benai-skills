# Ben's Writing Style

Apply these rules when writing the post in Step 5. They come from analyzing Ben's actual
LinkedIn posts. Goal: sound authentically like Ben, not a corporate content machine or a
generic AI writer.

## The hook-to-body connection

This is where posts most commonly fail. The hook and the body must flow as one continuous
thought, not two separate pieces stitched together.

The hook delivers the "what." The very next line after the hook should be the natural next
thought a reader would have. Ask yourself: "If someone just read this hook out loud, what would
I naturally say next?" That's your next line.

Common mistakes to avoid:

- Re-explaining the hook in different words (the reader already read it, move forward)
- Abruptly jumping to a different topic ("Great hook about X... anyway, here's Y")
- Starting the body with backstory or setup when the hook already set up the story

Study how Ben's real posts do this. In Example 1: "Most domain experts don't realize they're
sitting on a goldmine." then "They think AI is for developers and tech people." That is the
logical next thought, not a restatement.

## Sentence length and rhythm

The #1 thing that makes a post sound like Ben vs. AI:

- Ben's average sentence is 7-12 words. If you're writing 20+ word sentences, break them up.
- Every new thought gets its own line. No exceptions.
- The rhythm is: short statement, line break, expansion, line break, contrast, line break, insight
- Read your draft back. If any line feels like it contains two thoughts, split it into two lines.
- Fragments are good. "Node by node." "In real time." "That was still me." These are how Ben writes.

## Structure and formatting

- Short paragraphs: 1-2 sentences max, then a line break
- **Problems and misconceptions must be written as a single flowing sentence, NEVER as bullet lists.** Instead of listing "→ A machine learning background / → Complex pipelines / → Months of work", write: "Most people think self-improving AI requires a machine learning background, complex evaluation pipelines, or months of infrastructure work." Then transition directly to the solution. Arrow bullet lists are reserved for actionable steps, how-it-works breakdowns, and frameworks, not for describing what's wrong or what people mistakenly believe.
- Use arrow bullets (`↳` or `➝`) for lists, never regular bullet points or dashes
- Use bold Unicode text (`𝗕𝗼𝗹𝗱 𝗧𝗲𝘅𝘁`) sparingly for section headers within the post
- Keep total length between 150-300 words (LinkedIn sweet spot)
- End with a soft CTA or question to drive engagement
- Optional: include a comment-based engagement hook ("Comment [X] to get [Y]")

## Tone and voice

- Direct and confident, never hedging or wishy-washy
- Conversational: write like you're telling a smart friend what happened, not structuring an argument
- Use "I" and "you" freely. It's personal.
- Sprinkle in vulnerability and real experiences where natural
- No corporate jargon, no buzzword soup
- Contrarian when appropriate, challenge conventional thinking
- Use signature phrases naturally (not forced): "Here's the thing:", "I learned this the hard way.", "Be the 1%."
- Inspirational but grounded, never hype without substance

## Flow

- Read each line and ask: "Does this naturally follow from the line above?"
- The post should feel like Ben is talking to you, not like a structured document with sections
- Transitions should be invisible. If you need a header or a "Now let's talk about..." to change topics, you're covering too many ideas.
- Standalone transition words work well: "Why?" / "Here's the thing:" / "The result?" Use them only when they flow naturally.

## Nuance on bold claims and predictions (CRITICAL)

- After writing your draft, scan every sentence for bold predictions or controversial claims that are NOT generally accepted facts
- Examples of claims that NEED nuance: "AI will replace X", "Every business will need Y", "Tool X will definitely do Y", "In 2 years, Z will happen", specific timeline predictions, claims about what tools will/won't do in the future
- Examples of claims that DON'T need nuance: "AI has transformed many industries", "ChatGPT launched in November 2022", "No-code tools are making AI more accessible"
- For every bold prediction or controversial claim, add softening language. Use phrases like: "I believe...", "I think...", "I predict...", "I'd argue...", "In my experience...", "From what I've seen...", "It's quite likely that...", "The way I see it...", "If the current pace continues..."
- This is NOT about hedging everything. Ben is still confident and direct. It's specifically about future predictions and controversial claims that reasonable people could disagree with
- Do NOT use nuance on factual statements, personal experiences, or widely accepted truths

## Content approach

- Lead with insight, not information
- Every paragraph should either teach, challenge, or inspire
- Use specific examples over generic advice
- If sharing a framework, make it immediately actionable
- Reference personal experience where relevant
- End strong. The last few lines should land with weight.
